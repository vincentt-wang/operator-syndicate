The Buying Committee: Who Actually Says Yes in an Enterprise Deal

By Vincent Wang · EvoScale Capital · 7 min read · June 2026

Ask a founder who the buyer is, and you usually hear one name: the champion who loved the demo. Ask Gartner, and the answer is a committee of five to sixteen people across as many as four functions, most of whom you will never meet. The deal you think you're winning on the strength of your product is being decided in rooms you're not in. Knowing exactly who sits in those rooms — and what each of them is afraid of — is the difference between a deal that closes and one that quietly dies in 'we decided to hold off.'

One champion is not a deal

Start with the shape of the room. Gartner's research on B2B buying finds that a complex purchase now runs through a buying group of roughly six to ten people, drawn from as many as four different functions, with the largest groups reaching well past a dozen. Your champion is one vote in that room, and rarely the deciding one.

And it gets harder. In a 2025 Gartner survey, 74% of B2B buyer teams showed 'unhealthy conflict' during the decision process. Read that plainly: the people who must agree to buy from you usually can't agree with each other first. The same body of research finds that when a group does reach genuine consensus, it is about 2.5 times more likely to land on a high-quality, low-regret purchase. Consensus, not your pitch, is the gate.

Why the deal really stalls

The instinct, when a deal goes quiet, is to read it as a product objection: a missing feature, a price worry. Usually it isn't. The hardest job in enterprise buying is not choosing you over a competitor — it is the group agreeing internally at all. Gartner describes buying as a set of 'jobs,' and 'consensus creation' is the one where momentum most often dies. A deal far more often loses to 'no decision' than to a rival.

That reframes what a founder is actually selling. You are not just selling a product to a buyer. You are handing a champion the ammunition to win an internal argument on your behalf — in meetings you will never attend. The decisive question stops being 'is my product good enough?' and becomes 'have I armed the one person inside who is fighting for me?'

The five people who decide (and what kills your deal with each)

Strip the org chart down and most enterprise deals turn on five archetypes. They rarely match job titles one-to-one, but every committee contains them. Map all five before you pitch, not after you stall.

1. The economic buyer — controls the budget, owns the final yes

Wants: a business outcome and a return they can defend upward, to their own boss.

Quietly kills your deal when: there is no clear ROI, or the purchase feels like a risk to their own number.

2. The champion — sells you internally when you're not there

Wants: a visible win they can put their name on.

Quietly kills your deal when: you don't arm them — no proof, no numbers, no narrative they can carry into a room you'll never enter.

3. The technical & user evaluator — judges whether it actually works

Wants: something that solves the problem without adding burden to their day.

Quietly kills your deal when: integration, security or workflow gaps surface late, after trust was already given.

4. Procurement & legal — controls terms, price and process

Wants: control over risk, price and a process that won't blow up later.

Quietly kills your deal when: there are compliance gaps, a painful onboarding, or no references a cautious buyer can lean on.

5. The blocker — quietly defends the status quo

Wants: no disruption to what already works, and no new risk on their plate.

Quietly kills your deal when: they are left unsurfaced — you never learn they exist until the deal is already dead.

The pattern across all five is the same: each carries a personal risk, not just a corporate one. The economic buyer risks their number; procurement risks a bad contract; the blocker risks the disruption. A cold vendor asks every one of them to take that risk on faith — which is exactly why cold deals stall.

Why a warm path beats a cold pitch here

This is the precise point where access changes the math. As we argued in The Access Problem, the scarce resource in B2B is not capital but a credible path to the people who decide. Map it onto the committee and the value gets concrete. Someone who has sat in the economic buyer's chair knows what that person needs to defend the decision upward. Someone who has been the blocker knows how to surface and neutralise the objection early, before it goes underground.

An introduction from a person the committee already trusts does more than book a meeting. It pre-answers the internal questions that would otherwise stall you for two quarters. That is the practical reason operator networks matter for enterprise B2B: the operators have played every seat at the table, so they can tell a founder which of the five to win first, and exactly what each one needs to hear.

The takeaway

The conclusion is uncomfortable for product-led founders: the best product loses to the better-mapped deal. Before your next enterprise pitch, draw the committee — all five — and ask, for each, 'what do they need to say yes, and what would make them quietly say no?' The teams that win enterprise revenue are the ones that treat the buying committee, not the demo, as the real surface they are selling to.
