The Price Was Set Before the Negotiation Started

Operator Insider · Tech M&A · 8 min read · September 2026

Founders prepare for an acquisition as if it were a negotiation about what the company is worth. In practice the number is roughly fixed in the first serious meeting, and every conversation after that is about something else entirely. What gets negotiated is not the price. It is which side carries each risk that diligence turns up, expressed in price.

Buyers do not value the company. They value what transfers.

A buyer's model has two columns that founders rarely separate. One is what the business earns today. The other is what it will still earn under a new owner, with a new reporting line, after the founder's last day. Only the second column is being bought. Anything that lives in the first column but not the second is a cost the buyer will have to rebuild, and it is deducted before the offer is written, not during the negotiation.

This is the mechanism behind the most common surprise in the whole process. A founder who personally holds the largest customer relationships, makes the pricing calls and owns the architecture has built a company that performs well and transfers badly. From the inside that reads as being indispensable. In the model it appears as a deduction, and the founder usually never sees the line.

The blunt version of the same point is that the more of a company runs through one person, the lower the price, and the relationship is close to linear.

The price moves for one reason only

After an indicative offer, the price almost never goes up, and it comes down in a single pattern. Diligence finds something that was not disclosed, the buyer assigns a cost to it, and the offer drops by that cost. Nobody re-values the business. Every reduction is a specific risk being handed back to the seller with a number attached.

Seeing it this way changes what a seller should do with the months before a process starts. The work that protects price is not building a better story. It is removing the items a buyer would otherwise find and charge for. A customer contract with a change-of-control clause. An employment arrangement nobody ever papered. Revenue recognised in a way the buyer's auditor will restate. Open-source licences nobody has mapped. Each of these carries a price, each price is set unilaterally by the buyer, and each one disappears completely if it is resolved before anyone goes looking.

Sellers reliably spend that window on the deck instead.

What actually breaks a deal is never the price

Deals rarely die over valuation, because valuation is the one disagreement both sides already know how to split. They die over three things. The first is people: the buyer works out that the two or three individuals who actually make the business run have no reason to stay, and no retention package can be built fast enough to matter. The second is consent: a material customer or a licensor has to approve the change of control, and asking them opens a conversation the seller cannot control. The third is the data room, and the problem there is never what is in it. It is one question asked twice and answered two different ways.

Mostly theirs
Whether the key people stay
By the time a buyer asks, the answer was already decided by how those people were treated over the previous three years. A package negotiated in week six does not move it.
Partly yours
Consents and clauses
The change-of-control terms were signed years ago. Which ones exist, and who would have to be asked, can be known long before a buyer finds out on your behalf.
Entirely yours
A data room that agrees with itself
The cheapest risk to remove and the one most often left in place. One question answered two ways ends more processes than any valuation gap.

Bar height indicates how much of each sits inside the seller's control, not how often each occurs.

The third one ends more processes than the first two together, and it is the only one entirely within the seller's control. A buyer who catches a single inconsistency stops trusting the rest of the room, and from that point the diligence is no longer about the company. It is about what else has not been said.

What this means for anyone underwriting an early deal

The practical use of all this is that it runs backwards. If the price at exit is set by how much of the business transfers without the founder, then that property is worth measuring years before anyone is selling, and it is measurable today. Who holds the top ten customer relationships, by name. Whether pricing is a document or one person's judgement. Whether a second person could defend a design decision in the architecture. Whether the contracts were written by someone who expected an acquirer's counsel to read them.

None of these four questions are about growth, and a company can score badly on all of them while growing quickly. That combination is common. It is the specific shape of a company that raises well and sells poorly.

Where EvoScale reads this

Operator-led investing means looking at the exit from the buyer's seat at the first meeting rather than the last. The question is not what a company could be worth. It is which parts of it would still be standing under new ownership, and whether the founder has been building those parts on purpose. A company that is easy to buy was not made easy at the end. It was made easy four years earlier, by someone who decided not to be the only person who knew something.

Every price cut after the first meeting is a risk being handed back to the seller with a number attached. The founders who get the best outcomes are not the ones who negotiate hardest. They are the ones who left fewer things for a buyer to find.
