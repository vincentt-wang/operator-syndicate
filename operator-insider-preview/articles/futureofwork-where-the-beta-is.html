Where the Beta Is

Operator Insider · FutureOfWork · 10 min read · September 2026
Operator Insider · FutureOfWork
Will Lin
Director, Michael Page Taiwan
The author works at Michael Page. The views here are his own.
LinkedIn profile

In recruitment you pick up one habit early. When a CV lands, you look at where the person has been before you look at what they did there.

That order looks backwards. Most people assume ability is read first and the market second. In practice the industry sets the range a person can be priced in before anyone reads what they did there.

Investing already has a name for this. Your return splits in two. One part comes from the market carrying you up, and that is Beta. The other part is what you earn by being better than the people beside you, and that is Alpha.

For twenty years, career advice has been almost entirely about Alpha. Get sharper. Get faster. Go deeper. Almost nobody talks about Beta. AI is the reason that now has to change.

AI shortened how long effort lasts

The question worth asking is what happens to a skill once everyone can reach it.

Alpha is built on two gaps: the information you have that others do not, and the fluency you have that others have not reached yet. Those are precisely the two gaps AI closes fastest. Beta is built on neither. Beta is the position you stand in, and your ability does not enter into it.

The turnover in required skills is measurable, and the two groups keep pulling apart. Comparing the most AI-exposed occupations against the least exposed, the gap in how much their skill mix changed ran at 25 per cent in 2023, 66 per cent in 2024 and 116 per cent in 2025.

A second measure, from the employer side, runs on a different clock and says something gentler. In the World Economic Forum’s survey, the share of core skills employers expect to change within five years peaked at 57 per cent in 2020, sat at 44 per cent in 2023 and 39 per cent in 2025. WEF’s own reading is that employers have adapted since the pandemic and that more workers have completed training, 50 per cent against 41. What employers expect is stabilising at a high level. What the postings measure keeps widening.

The skill belongs to you. What a market will pay to add it belongs to the market. These are eight internal gaps, each measured against its own baseline. The absolute salaries across those industries are a separate question.

Taiwan is not in that dataset, so the local figures come from a separate survey by 104 Job Bank. Among 909 company HR respondents, 75 per cent said they would offer more, in pay or other incentives, for AI development skills. The pay premium they named averaged above 9.5 per cent and ran from 3 to 35 per cent depending on the industry. Note what that survey measures: what employers say they are willing to add. Whether it landed in anyone’s account, it does not say.

What choosing an industry buys you

In 2024, the median annual pay in finance and insurance was NT$1.106m. In education it was NT$382,000. That education figure carries a label in the table’s own heading: it excludes every public and private school from primary level up, so it is cram schools, kindergartens and tutoring centres, and schoolteachers are outside it. That is a gap of NT$723,000 between two industries, and it is the number that usually ends the discussion.

Inside finance and insurance, the distance from the tenth percentile to the ninetieth is NT$1.518m, more than twice the gap between the two industries. Inside education it is NT$281,000, under four tenths of it.

Two things about that spread have to be said before it carries any weight. The population behind the table is every paid employee in industry and services, full-time and part-time, local and foreign, so one distribution holds the counter clerk and the trader, the graduate and the veteran. How much of finance’s NT$1.518m is job level and tenure, and how much is the difference between doing the job well and doing it adequately, the table does not separate. What it shows is how far the ceiling sits from the floor. Whether effort alone covers that distance, it cannot show.

Education’s flattened bar needs its own reading. The tenth, twentieth and thirtieth percentiles there are the identical figure, NT$329,640, which is twelve months of the 2024 minimum monthly wage of NT$27,470, and the all-employee tenth percentile is the same figure. Somewhere between two and four in ten people in that column sit on exactly that number. The statistics office does not explain how the cluster forms, and in earlier years the two figures differed, so the bottom of that column says little about effort either way.

So whether Beta beats Alpha depends on which square you are standing in, and the honest version of the claim is narrower than it first sounds. Choosing an industry does not buy you a higher median. It buys you a distribution whose ceiling sits further from the floor. Beta does not replace Alpha. Beta sets how much room there is. Whether your Alpha fills it is a question the table leaves open.

The ninetieth percentile in education is NT$610,000. The thirtieth percentile in publishing, media and ICT is NT$611,000. Reach the top ten per cent of one industry and you have landed in the bottom thirty per cent of the other. The effort was real. It was priced in a market that had less room to price it. Run the same reading against manufacturing and the answer changes: manufacturing’s ninetieth percentile, NT$1.397m, lands around the sixty-sixth percentile of finance, inside the top third, and manufacturing’s own spread of NT$1.030m is 1.9 times its NT$536,000 gap to finance. Which number is bigger, the gap between industries or the spread inside one, changes with the square you are standing in.

There is still a cost asymmetry, and it is the reason position deserves the first question. Crossing an industry boundary is quick. What it costs is someone on the other side saying yes. Climbing the equivalent distance on qualifications takes years, plus the income you give up while you do it. AI does not change either cost. It shortens how long the expensive one stays valid before it needs rebuilding.

So, depth or breadth

Going deep bets that the field stays valuable long enough, and that you can reach the top of it. Going wide bets that the overlap between two fields is scarce, and that the overlap keeps widening. Those are different bets. The ambition behind them is the same.

Depth pays, on the 22 per cent track. Getting onto that track is the hard part. Most roles sit on the other one, where the same effort buys a flatter slope.

Group American occupations by how much preparation they demand and the pay steps between the groups tell a second story about depth. From the middle group to the one above it, the median pay step is 48 per cent. From there to the most demanding group it is 27 per cent on the median, and the largest step of all on the mean. Both are true at once: the typical person at the top gains the smallest step, while the average is pulled up by a few who gain a great deal. That is a comparison between occupations. One person’s climb may run differently. It still points the same way: the top layer is where the spread widens, and where Alpha finally starts to matter more than position.

The other track has its own number. In 2024, 51 per cent of postings that asked for AI skills sat outside IT and computer science occupations, on Lightcast’s count. The same firm puts the AI pay premium at 28 per cent across 1.3 billion postings. PwC, working from Lightcast’s data, gets 62. Different method, twice the size, so read both as direction. Half of the postings asking for the overlap sit in occupations outside IT.

Among hiring managers in Michael Page’s Taiwan survey, adaptability came first at 44 per cent, ahead of communication and continuous learning at 36 per cent each. Industry-specific technical expertise, which sat second at 39 per cent the year before, did not make the top three. The survey had 592 Taiwanese respondents, 52 per cent of them with management responsibilities. The size of the hiring-manager subsample is not published. Talent Trends is my own firm’s survey, and a finding that adaptability outranks depth is convenient for a recruiter, so weigh it accordingly. Depth is still valued. Employers are asking a different question first, and the question is how quickly you change.

Both bets can be losing quietly for years, because effort keeps feeling like progress. A rank that does not move when you do is the earliest signal either route gives you. The pay portal publishes the decile table for every year back to 2012, so the deep bet can be checked against your own history: three years of total pay, each read against that year’s table. The wide bet is checked the same way, with one figure read against two fields.

Four questions, and which one is the Beta question

I put these four questions to a room of university students earlier this year. In my trade you also meet plenty of people with real tenure who cannot answer them, and the ones who do not know what they want outnumber the ones who do.

Do I enjoy this? Am I good at this? Am I willing to get better at it? And does it create value for a market or for other people?

The first three are Alpha questions. Only the fourth one asks about Beta, and it is the only one that is not about you.

None of this argues for abandoning depth. Depth is what the 22 per cent track is made of, and the spread at the top of a field is where the largest individual returns still live. It argues for something smaller and more practical: run the fourth question first, because it is the cheapest one to act on and the one that quietly sets the ceiling on the other three.

Effort decides how fast you swim. Position decides where you end up.

The weakest number in this piece, named by us before someone else does

The weakest step in this piece is reading the spread inside an industry as room for effort. The table’s population is every paid employee in industry and services, full-time and part-time, local and foreign, on one distribution. How much of finance’s NT$1.518m is job level and tenure, and how much is the difference between doing the job well and doing it adequately, the table does not break down. Stripping out part-time and foreign staff does not shrink it either: the tenth-to-ninetieth spread for full-time Taiwanese employees, NT$1.040m, is slightly wider than the all-employee NT$1.006m. So the spread is real, and what it is made of is unknown.

The second weakest is the fallback we had ready: that finance against manufacturing makes the same point as finance against education. We ran it. Manufacturing’s ninetieth percentile lands around the sixty-sixth percentile of finance, the top third, and manufacturing’s own spread is 1.9 times its gap to finance. Manufacturing’s distribution is not flattened at all.

Nor is the flattened shape only an education-column artefact. Across the 47 industries in the same table, 16 have an internal spread smaller than their gap to the finance median, and in textiles and metal products the tenth percentile is not pinned to the minimum-wage figure. So the conclusion that survives is smaller than the headline. Some industries keep the ceiling far from the floor and some keep it close, and the table tells you which one you are in. Whether that distance is a return on effort, this table cannot show, and the piece leans on it as if it could.

What to do this week, for nothing

None of this needs a budget, a mentor or a plan. It needs one afternoon and a published table.

Take last year’s total pay, the annual figure with bonuses in, and read it against the official decile table for your own industry. That tells you where you sit among people in the same industry. Then read the same figure against one adjacent industry, the one you could plausibly move into without retraining. The second reading tells you whether your number counts as front or back of the pack over there. It does not tell you what you would be paid there, because that depends on how the other side prices you. The next step is three postings for your function in that industry, and where their pay ranges sit on that industry’s distribution.

Where EvoScale reads this

An operator-led fund reads talent the way other people read filings. Where senior people are moving, and which functions a company has started paying up for, both tend to show before any of it reaches a financial statement. A company will redraft a forecast in an afternoon. Job adverts can mislead too: anyone in this trade knows the ghost posting, and every platform carries them. A single advert proves little. The same function moving in the same direction for several months, with the offered pay moving with it, is harder to fake.

If an industry’s Beta is rising, its hiring shows it first. That makes one mistake easy to make from our side of the table: reading a hot sector as a strong company. A rising Beta lifts the mediocre operator in that sector too, and hiring data does not tell you which one you are looking at. A fund that reads talent first is exactly the fund most exposed to that error.
