Where the Beta Is

Operator Insider · FutureOfWork · 10 min read · September 2026
Operator Insider · FutureOfWork
Will Lin
Director, Michael Page Taiwan
LinkedIn profile

In recruitment you pick up one habit early. When a CV lands, you look at where the person has been before you look at what they did there.

That order looks wrong. Most people assume you read the ability first and the market second. You do not. The same ability, priced in two different industries, opens a wider gap than the gap between doing that job well and doing it adequately in the same place.

Investing already has a name for this. Your return splits in two. One part comes from the market carrying you up, and that is Beta. The other part is what you earn by being better than the people beside you, and that is Alpha.

For twenty years, career advice has been almost entirely about Alpha. Get sharper. Get faster. Go deeper. Almost nobody talks about Beta. AI is the reason that now has to change.

AI did not make effort cheaper. It shortened how long effort lasts.

The question worth asking is not whether AI will replace you. It is what happens to a skill once everyone can reach it.

Alpha is built on two gaps: the information you have that others do not, and the fluency you have that others have not reached yet. Those are precisely the two gaps AI closes fastest. Beta is not built on either. Beta is not your ability at all. It is the position you stand in.

The turnover in required skills is measurable, and it is accelerating. Comparing the most AI-exposed occupations against the least exposed, the gap in how much their skill mix changed ran at 25 percent in 2023, 66 percent in 2024 and 116 percent in 2025.

One detail is worth holding on to. Over the same period, employers reported feeling calmer about skill disruption, not more anxious: the share of core skills they expect to change within five years fell from 44 percent to 39 percent. What people feel is settling down. What the job postings actually show is speeding up.

Taiwan is not in that dataset, so we asked the local version separately. Among 909 Taiwanese employers surveyed, 75 percent said they would pay more for AI development skills, and the premium they named ranged from 3 percent to 35 percent depending on the industry. Note what that survey measures: what employers say they are willing to add, not what landed in anyone's account.

What choosing an industry actually buys you

If the argument stopped at the chart above it would read as a pitch for switching industries, and the Taiwanese numbers say something more interesting than that.

Start with the gap everyone quotes. In 2024, the median annual pay in finance and insurance was NT$1.106m. In education it was NT$382,000. That education figure needs a label the table carries and most people never read: it excludes every public and private school above primary level, so it is cram schools, kindergartens and tutoring centres, not teachers. That is a gap of NT$723,000 between two industries, and it is the number that usually ends the discussion.

Now look inside each of them instead.

One caveat: the first three deciles in education are the identical figure, so the spread shown for education is a floor, not a precise value.

Inside finance and insurance, the distance from the tenth percentile to the ninetieth is NT$1.518m. That is more than twice the gap between the two industries. Inside education it is NT$281,000, under four tenths of it. Same table, same year, same definition.

So the honest answer to whether Beta beats Alpha is that it depends entirely on which square you are standing in. In finance, the spread inside the industry dwarfs the gap between industries, and Alpha is the main event. In education the spread looks flattened, but the reason is worth reading properly: the tenth, twentieth and thirtieth percentiles are all the identical figure, NT$329,640, which is NT$27,470 a month. The all-employee tenth percentile is that same NT$329,640. When two different populations land on the same number to the last digit, that is an institutional floor, not a feature of the industry. Three in ten people in that column are sitting on it.

Which turns the whole question around. Choosing an industry does not buy you a higher median. It buys you a distribution in which effort still counts. Beta is not a substitute for Alpha. Beta sets how much room your Alpha has to work in.

One line from that chart makes it concrete. The ninetieth percentile in education is NT$610,000. The thirtieth percentile in publishing, media and ICT is NT$611,000. Reach the top ten percent of one industry and you have landed in the bottom thirty percent of the other. The effort was real. It was priced in a market that had less room to price it.

There is a cost asymmetry underneath all of this that is worth naming. Moving across that boundary is one decision, made inside a quarter, needing nobody's permission. Climbing the equivalent distance through qualifications is six to eight years and the income you gave up to get them. AI does not change either multiple. It shortens how long the expensive one stays valid before it needs rebuilding.

So, depth or breadth

This is the part where most articles pick a side. This one will not, because the evidence does not.

Start with what each choice is actually betting on. Going deep bets that the field stays valuable long enough, and that you can reach the top of it. Going wide bets that the overlap between two fields is scarce, and that the overlap widens rather than closes. Those are different bets, not different levels of ambition.

Depth pays, and the chart says so. But it pays on the 22 percent track, and getting there is not a matter of deciding to. Most roles sit on the other track, where the same effort buys a flatter slope.

There is a second thing about depth that rarely gets said. Measured across American occupations, the median pay step from capable to genuinely specialised is 48 percent. The next step, from specialised to the very top of a field, is 27 percent. The marginal return on depth shrinks at exactly the point where the work gets hardest. What widens instead is the spread inside that top layer, which is another way of saying that the deepest layer is where Alpha finally starts to matter more than position.

Now the other side. In 2024, 51 percent of postings requiring AI skills sat outside IT and information science altogether. Cross-domain has stopped being a bonus line on a CV. It has become the default way a normal role in a normal function is now written.

Which is roughly what employers here have started saying out loud. Among 592 Taiwanese respondents surveyed in late 2025, adaptability came first at 44 percent, ahead of communication at 36 percent. Industry-specific technical expertise, which sat second at 39 percent the year before, did not make the top three at all. Depth has not stopped being valuable. Employers are simply asking a different question first, and the question is how quickly you change.

The scarcest talent of the future is not the most experienced person. It is the person who can distil wisdom out of that experience, and then multiply it through communication and collaboration.

Brad P., at a Michael Page Talent Trends Lunch, June 2026
Read next to the numbers above, that line stops being a nice sentiment. Experience is depth. Distilling and multiplying it is what carries across a boundary.

Four questions, and which one is the Beta question

I have used the same four questions with students and with people twenty years into a career. They work in both rooms, which is usually a sign the questions are doing real work.

Do I enjoy this. Am I good at this. Am I willing to get better at it. And does it create value for a market or for other people.

Put those four next to everything above and the shape changes. The first three are all Alpha questions. Only the fourth one asks about Beta, and it is the one people skip, because it is the only one that is not about them.

None of this argues for abandoning depth. Depth is what the 22 percent track is made of, and the spread at the top of a field is where the largest individual returns still live. It argues for something smaller and more practical: run the fourth question first, because it is the cheapest one to act on and the one that quietly sets the ceiling on the other three.

Effort decides how fast you swim. Position decides where you end up.

The weakest number in this piece, named by us before someone else does

The education column is the one doing the most work in this article, and it is the one a reader should push back on hardest.

The official table excludes every public and private school above primary level. What is left is cram schools, kindergartens and tutoring centres. So when this piece says a teacher at the top of education earns what someone in the bottom third of ICT earns, it is not talking about teachers at all. We flagged it on the chart, but the honest version is blunter: that column should probably not be labelled education.

The argument survives without it. Finance against manufacturing makes the same point with no caveat attached, and the decile spread inside finance does not depend on the education row at all. We kept the education column because the flattened distribution is genuinely unusual and worth showing. Read it as one industry classification behaving oddly, not as a statement about anyone’s teacher.

What to do this week, for nothing

None of this needs a budget, a mentor or a plan. It needs one afternoon and a published table.

Take last year’s total pay, the figure on your tax filing rather than your monthly salary, and read it against the official decile table for your own industry. That tells you where you sit among people doing roughly what you do. Then read the same figure against one adjacent industry, the one you could plausibly move into without retraining. If the two positions are close, your industry is not the constraint and the work is to go deeper. If they are far apart, you have just measured your Beta, and the number is larger than most people assume.

The calculator above does that in about a minute. Doing it by hand on the published table takes ten, and you will remember it longer.

Where EvoScale reads this

An operator-led fund reads talent the way other people read filings. Where senior people are moving, and which functions a company has started paying up for, both show up months before any of it reaches a financial statement. A company will redraft a forecast. Opening a role it does not need costs considerably more, though anyone in this trade knows the ghost posting exists and every platform carries them. So the signal is never a single advert. It is the same function moving in the same direction for several months.

Which makes the same chart useful from the other side of the table. If an industry's Beta is rising, its hiring shows it first. It also makes one mistake easy to name, because we have made it: reading a hot sector as a strong company. A rising Beta lifts the mediocre operator in that sector too, and the hiring data does not tell you which one you are looking at.
