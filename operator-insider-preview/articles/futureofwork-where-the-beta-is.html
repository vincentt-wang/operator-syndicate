Where the Beta Is

Operator Insider · FutureOfWork
Will Lin
Director, Michael Page Taiwan

LinkedIn profile

Stay in recruitment long enough and you pick up one habit. When a CV lands, you look at where the person has been these past few years before you look at what they did there.

That order looks counterintuitive. We tend to assume ability or experience is read first and the market second. In practice the industry sets our starting point before anyone reads what we did there and what we left behind.

Investing already has a clear name for this. Your return splits in two. One part comes from the market carrying you up, and that is Beta. The other part is what you earn by doing better than everyone else, and that is Alpha.

For twenty years, career advice has been almost entirely about Alpha. Growth mindset. Specialisation. Almost nobody talks about Beta. AI is the reason we now have to.

The question worth asking is how much a skill is still worth once the opportunity cost of acquiring it falls.

Alpha is built on two gaps: what you know that others do not, and what you have mastered that others have not yet. Those are precisely the two gaps AI is best at closing. Beta is built on neither. Beta is the position you stand in and chose to stand in. It does not track your ability all that closely. It tracks your eye and your judgement.

How fast the skills the market asks for turn over can be calculated, and the gap between the two groups grows every year. Comparing the occupations most connected to AI against the least, the gap in how much their skill mix changed ran at 25 per cent in 2023, 66 per cent in 2024 and 116 per cent in 2025.

What choosing an industry buys you

The skill is yours. How much gets added for it is the market's call. In 2024 the median annual pay in finance and insurance was NT$1.106m and in education NT$382,000; that column excludes schools at every level, so it is cram schools, tutoring centres and kindergartens. The gap is NT$723,000, and most comparisons of industries stop there.

Inside finance and insurance, the 10th to 90th percentile spread is NT$1.518m, more than twice the gap between the two industries; inside education it is NT$281,000, under four tenths of that. Education's bar is flattened: its 10th, 20th and 30th percentiles are one figure, twelve months of the minimum monthly wage, so two to four in ten people in that column sit on exactly that number, and the bottom of the bar has nothing to do with effort.

What choosing an industry really buys is a distribution whose ceiling sits further from the floor. The median is secondary. Beta sets how wide the pay band is; Alpha sets where in it you land. Education's 90th percentile is NT$610,000 and ICT's 30th percentile is NT$611,000: reach the top 10 per cent of one industry and you rank in the bottom 30 per cent next door. The effort was real. The market pricing it had no room.

Crossing an industry line is quick; the price is that someone on the other side has to say yes, and the side hiring you sets that price. Climbing the same distance inside your own industry on qualifications takes years, plus the income you give up. AI changes neither cost. It only shortens how long the expensive one stays valid, which is why position comes first.

Depth, or breadth

Depth pays inside the 22 per cent cell; most roles sit in the 52 per cent cell. Among Taiwan hiring managers in Michael Page's Talent Trends, adaptability ranked first at 44 per cent, and industry-specific technical expertise, second the year before, fell out of the top three. This is my own firm's survey. Depth is still valued. Employers just ask another question first: how fast you change.

Brad’s line, as I wrote it down afterwards: the scarcest talent of the future is the person who can distil wisdom out of experience and then multiply it through communication and collaboration, and that person is a different one from the most experienced in the room.

Four questions, and which one asks about Beta

I put these four questions to university students as a guest lecturer earlier this year. People with real tenure often cannot answer them either, and those who do not know what they want far outnumber those who do.

Do I enjoy this? Am I good at this? Am I willing to get better at it? And does it create value for a market or for other people?

The first three are Alpha questions. Only the fourth asks about Beta, and it is the only one not about you.

Depth still pays: it is what the 22 per cent cell is made of, and the top of a field is still where the largest returns sit. Ask the fourth question first: it is the cheapest and quickest to act on, and it sets the ceiling on the other three.

Position sets the ceiling. Effort decides how far you get under it.

Do both, in this order: check whether the square you stand in is worth standing in, then ask whether you can get to the front of it. Where senior people move, and which functions a company starts paying up for, show earlier than any filing and read truer than any CV. Before the next pay conversation, read your own number against the decile table once.
