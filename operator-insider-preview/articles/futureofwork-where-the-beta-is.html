Where the Beta Is

Operator Insider · FutureOfWork
Will Lin
Director, Michael Page Taiwan

LinkedIn profile

Stay in recruitment long enough and you pick up one habit. When a CV lands, you look at where the person has been these past few years before you look at what they did there.

That order looks counterintuitive. We tend to assume ability or experience is read first and the market second. In practice the industry sets our starting point before anyone reads what we did there and what we left behind.

Investing already has a clear name for this. Your return splits in two. One part comes from the market carrying you up, and that is Beta. The other part is what you earn by doing better than everyone else, and that is Alpha.

For twenty years, career advice has been almost entirely about Alpha. Growth mindset. Specialisation. Almost nobody talks about Beta. AI is the reason we now have to.

AI shortened how long effort lasts

The question worth asking is how much a skill is still worth once the opportunity cost of acquiring it falls.

Alpha is built on two gaps: what you know that others do not, and what you have mastered that others have not yet. Those are precisely the two gaps AI is best at closing. Beta is built on neither. Beta is the position you stand in and chose to stand in. It does not track your ability all that closely. It tracks your eye and your judgement.

How fast the skills the market asks for turn over can be calculated, and the gap between the two groups grows every year. Comparing the occupations most connected to AI against the least, the gap in how much their skill mix changed ran at 25 per cent in 2023, 66 per cent in 2024 and 116 per cent in 2025.

What choosing an industry buys you

In 2024, the median annual pay in finance and insurance was NT$1.106m. In education it was NT$382,000, and that figure excludes every school from primary level up, so it is cram schools, tutoring centres and kindergartens. That is a gap of NT$723,000, and it is where most discussions end.

Inside finance and insurance, the distance from the tenth percentile to the ninetieth is NT$1.518m, more than twice the gap between the two industries. Inside education it is NT$281,000, under four tenths of it.

Education's flattened bar needs its own reading. Its tenth, twentieth and thirtieth percentiles are the identical figure, twelve months of the minimum monthly wage, so between two and four in ten people in that column sit on exactly that number, and the bottom of the bar says little about effort either way.

So whether Beta beats Alpha depends on which square you are standing in. What choosing an industry really buys you is a distribution whose ceiling sits further from the floor. The median is secondary. Beta does not replace Alpha. It sets how much room there is, and whether your Alpha fills it is a question the table cannot answer.

The ninetieth percentile in education is NT$610,000. The thirtieth percentile in publishing, media and ICT is NT$611,000. Reach the top ten per cent of one industry and you land in the bottom thirty per cent of the one next door. The effort was real. It was priced in a market with little room to price it.

The two costs are very different, which is why position gets asked first. Crossing an industry boundary is quick. What it costs is someone on the other side saying yes. Climbing the same distance on qualifications takes years, plus the income you give up. AI does not change either cost. It only shortens how long the expensive one stays valid.

Depth pays, on the 22 per cent track. Getting onto that track is the hard part. Most roles sit on the other one, where the same effort buys a flatter slope.

Among hiring managers in Michael Page's Taiwan survey, adaptability came first at 44 per cent, and industry-specific technical expertise, second the year before, did not make the top three. Talent Trends is my own firm's survey, the hiring-manager subsample is not published, and this finding is convenient for a recruiter, so weigh it accordingly. Depth is still valued. Employers are just asking a different question first: how quickly you change.

Four questions, and which one is the Beta question

I put these four questions to a room of university students earlier this year. People with real tenure often cannot answer them either, and the ones who do not know what they want outnumber the ones who do.

Do I enjoy this? Am I good at this? Am I willing to get better at it? And does it create value for a market or for other people?

The first three are Alpha questions. Only the fourth one asks about Beta, and it is the only one that is not about you.

None of this argues against depth. Depth is what the 22 per cent track is made of, and the top of a field is still where the largest individual returns live. Run the fourth question first: it is the cheapest and quickest to act on, and it quietly sets the ceiling on the other three.

Effort decides how fast you swim. Position decides where you end up.

The weakest number in this piece, named by us before someone else does

The weakest step in this piece is reading the spread inside an industry as room for effort. How much of finance's NT$1.518m is job level and tenure, and how much is the difference between doing the job well and doing it adequately, the table does not break down. Stripping out part-time and foreign staff does not shrink it either: the tenth-to-ninetieth spread for full-time Taiwanese employees, NT$1.040m, is slightly wider than the all-employee NT$1.006m. The spread is real. What it is made of is unknown.

The second weakest is the fallback we kept for ourselves: that finance against manufacturing should make the same point as finance against education. We ran it. It does not. Manufacturing's ninetieth percentile lands around the sixty-sixth percentile of finance, and manufacturing's own spread is 1.9 times its gap to finance. Manufacturing's distribution is not flattened at all.

Nor is the flattened shape only an education-column artefact: across the 47 industries in the same table, 16 have an internal spread smaller than their gap to the finance median, and not all of them are pinned to the minimum wage. The conclusion that survives is smaller than the headline: some ceilings sit far from the floor and some sit close, and the table tells you which one you are in. Whether that distance is a return on effort, the table cannot show, and this piece leans on it as if it could.

What to do this week, for nothing

Take last year's total pay with bonuses in, and read it against the official decile table for your own industry. That tells you where you sit among people in the same industry. Then read the same figure against one adjacent industry, the one you could move into without retraining. The second reading tells you whether your number counts as front or back of the pack over there. It does not tell you what you would be paid there, because that depends on how the other side prices you.

Where EvoScale reads this

An operator-led fund reads talent the way other people read filings. Where senior people are moving, and which functions companies have started paying up for, tend to show before any of it reaches a financial statement. A forecast can be redrafted in an afternoon, and some job adverts are ghost postings. The same function moving in the same direction for several months, with the offered pay moving with it, is harder to fake.

If an industry's Beta is rising, its hiring shows it first. That makes one mistake easy to make from our side of the table: reading a hot sector as a strong company. A rising Beta lifts the mediocre operator too, and hiring data does not tell you which one you are looking at.
