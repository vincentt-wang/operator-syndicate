The Enterprise AI Budget Has a Sales Line. It Is Small.

In Menlo's 2025 report, enterprise AI budgets do have a sales line: US$390m, about 5 per cent of departmental spend. Then usage pricing asks the buyer for a harder number: what next year costs, and who signs under it.

Sales has a line, and 78% of it is not with the platforms

Menlo's 2025 report puts enterprise spending on generative AI at US$37bn, of which US$7.3bn sits inside departmental budgets. Split nine ways, sales comes fifth at US$390m, about 5 per cent of departmental spend, and roughly 1 per cent of the headline.

Menlo puts 78 per cent with startups, and the reason it gives is workflow the platforms do not cover: research, personalisation and enrichment. The author works at Salesforce. Put those shares back through the same report and the incumbent side is well under US$100m, with about US$300m on the startup side.

The adoption number is one percent

The Bridge Group's tenth SDR metrics report covers 351 B2B companies. AI SDRs appear as a category for the first time at 1 per cent of respondents. In the same report 60 per cent of SDRs hit quota, the lowest since the study began and falling since 2018.

The share of reps making quota is the lowest on record, and the technology said to be replacing them is in use at one company in a hundred. One per cent of 351 is three or four firms. Every sales leader has reason to want it. Ninety-nine in a hundred have not bought it.

Of the three, the number that shows whether the buyer's budget line stayed put is net revenue retention. ZoomInfo's 89 per cent in Q2 2026 means its existing customers together paid 11 per cent less than a year earlier. Retention is the decision made at renewal, and it sits closer to the CFO's view than any new contract.

The most repeated prediction is that seat pricing is ending, and the evidence does not support it yet. Agentforce ARR went from US$800m to US$1.2bn in a quarter, and on the same call seven of the ten largest deals added new seats. That is the company talking to investors, and the author works there. Both pricing models grew in the same quarter.

In April 2026 HubSpot moved its service agent to US$0.50 per resolution. Its Q4 average subscription revenue per customer was US$11,683, about US$974 a month, which the agent reaches at roughly 1,950 resolutions; past that point the bill is larger than an average full subscription. With no cap in place, that is how a usage line crosses a seat line.

Budgeting for a subscription and budgeting for consumption are two different jobs

A subscription is a decision made once a year by one person. Usage is a forecast, and a forecast needs an owner. A budget line that moves with usage needs a number beside it for the year, and someone willing to sign under that number. That is where a buyer who believes the product works still stops.

A usage contract has a moment the sales process never rehearses: the first invoice, and whether it matches the business case. The way through is to put the ceiling on the table before the buyer asks. A cap is not a concession. It is what makes the purchase approvable at all.

What the mailbox rules cover, and what a customer logo proves

The claim that AI floods inboxes with outbound is half right. From February 2024 Google set thresholds for senders of more than 5,000 messages a day to personal Gmail, but its own guidance says those rules do not apply to mail sent to Workspace accounts, and B2B outbound goes to company domains. For a product whose value rests on volume, the question is which inbox it sends to.

In March 2025 TechCrunch reported that ZoomInfo and Airtable appeared on 11x's customer page while neither was a customer, and ZoomInfo's spokesperson said the product had performed well below their own SDR staff during the trial. Every logo on a customer page gets the same question: paying, trialling, or one conversation. A founder with nothing to hide answers it the first time.

Treat adoption claims as claims: the only one here backed by a named survey is that 1 per cent. Then ask which budget line the money comes from, because that answer is who signs.

The number that decides who wins is the one the buyer takes to the CFO

This market is real and someone will win it. Not whoever tells the best outcome story, but whoever lets the buyer work out the most this can cost in a year. Sell the ceiling before the outcome: a buyer who cannot cost it cannot get it approved. Before the next meeting, write the annual ceiling as one number and take it with you.
