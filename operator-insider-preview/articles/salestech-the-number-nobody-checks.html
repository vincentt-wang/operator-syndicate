The Enterprise AI Budget Has a Sales Line. It Is Small.

Sales is fifth of nine categories, at US$390 million

Menlo's 2025 report puts enterprise generative AI spend at US$37 billion, up from US$11.5 billion in 2024. US$7.3 billion of that sits inside department budgets, and Menlo's chart splits it nine ways: product and engineering US$4.2 billion, IT US$700 million, marketing US$660 million, customer success US$630 million, sales US$390 million, HR US$360 million, data science US$240 million, finance and operations US$100 million, market research under US$100 million. Sales is fifth of nine, about 5% of what departments spent.

Put another way, of every hundred dollars a department spent on AI in 2025, about five went to sales tools and about fifty-eight went to product and engineering. Against the total of US$37 billion, sales is about 1%. The line is there. It is just much smaller than the category is usually described.

Startups hold 78% of it

Menlo also splits each category between startups and incumbents. For sales it gives startups 78%, and names the reason: AI-native companies like Clay and Actively win by attacking workflows Salesforce does not own, meaning research, personalisation and enrichment. The author of this piece works at Salesforce. The line for sales exists. It is small, and most of it is being spent outside the platforms.

Menlo's split, applied to Menlo's own number, divides the sales line into roughly US$300 million for startups and roughly US$86 million for incumbents. For a platform vendor, the enterprise sales AI budget in this report is a market of well under US$100 million. For a startup, the same arithmetic says the money in play is about US$300 million, and the buyers in this report already put most of it with companies like it.

What the survey cannot say is why the line is small. The reading offered here starts from how the tools are priced. A seat is a fixed annual figure. A tool priced by usage is a forecast, and a forecast needs an owner. Menlo's data cannot test that reading. The pricing changes and retention figures the companies themselves have published can, at least in part.

The adoption number is one percent

The Bridge Group's tenth SDR metrics report, published in February 2025, covers 351 B2B companies. AI SDRs appeared as a distinct category for the first time, at 1% of respondents. In the same report, the share of SDRs hitting quota was 60%, the lowest since the study began, on a line that has trended down since 2018.

Those two numbers sit oddly together. The share of reps making quota is at a record low, and the technology said to be replacing them is in one company out of a hundred. One percent of 351 is three or four companies. Whatever is pressing on sales development teams, the survey does not show AI taking the work yet.

Taken together, the two numbers put the buyer's side in focus. Four reps in ten are missing quota, and the line has been going that way for seven years. A sales leader looking at that has every reason to want the tool, and ninety-nine companies in a hundred still had not bought one.

The number that separates Clay from ZoomInfo is retention

In 2025 and 2026 the category split three ways.

Gong is growing and still repriced. The company says ARR passed US$500 million in May 2026, with more than 5,000 customers and growth over 55% year on year; those are its own figures. Its 2021 Series E valued it at US$7.25 billion. Calcalist reported in November 2025 that a secondary round for employees and investors, then in advanced stages, priced it near US$4.5 billion, a discount of about 38%. Gong has not confirmed it.

ZoomInfo is losing altitude. FY2025 revenue of US$1.2495 billion, up 3%, with net revenue retention at 90% at year end. In May 2026 it cut guidance and announced a reduction of about 600 roles, about 20% of the company, and the stock fell 32.8% the day after the results. By the second quarter of 2026 net revenue retention was 89%. On 31 July 2026 its market capitalization, taking the closing price and the share count in its 10-Q, was about US$961 million, less than one times revenue.

Clay went the other way. From US$1 million to US$100 million ARR in two years. A tender offer in January 2026 priced the company at US$5 billion, with 14,000 customers. On 9 September 2026 it raised a Series D at a US$7.1 billion valuation, with more than 17,000 customers, after what it reports as 4x revenue growth in 2025. Same year, same category, three different outcomes.

Of the three, the number that says whether a buyer's budget line stayed put is net revenue retention. ZoomInfo's 89% in the second quarter of 2026 means its existing customers, taken together, paid 11% less than a year earlier. Clay reports enterprise net revenue retention above 200%, its own figure. Gong has not published one. Retention is a decision buyers make at renewal, and it sits closer to what a CFO thinks than any newly signed contract.

The valuations, on the figures the companies and Calcalist have put out, show how differently a dollar of revenue is priced in this category. Gong at about US$4.5 billion against more than US$500 million of ARR is roughly nine times. ZoomInfo at US$961 million against US$1.25 billion of revenue is under 0.8 times. Same category, a tenfold difference, and the only one of the two that has published retention is the one priced under 0.8 times, with existing customers paying 11% less.

Seats did not collapse

The most repeated prediction about AI and sales is that per seat pricing is finished. The evidence does not support it yet. Salesforce closed FY26 with revenue of US$41.5 billion and Agentforce ARR of US$800 million, up 169%. One quarter later, in Q1 FY27 reported on 27 May 2026, Agentforce ARR reached US$1.2 billion, up 205%, and on that call president and chief revenue officer Miguel Milano said seven of the top ten deals added new seats. That is the company's account to its investors, and the author works there.

Side by side, the two quarters say something else. Agentforce went from US$800 million to US$1.2 billion of ARR in one quarter, a 50% step, and in the same quarter the largest deals were still adding seats. The usage product and the seat product grew at the same company in the same quarter. Nothing in those two quarters of reporting shows one replacing the other.

What is changing is slower. HubSpot's customer count grew 16% in 2025, to 288,706, while average subscription revenue per customer in the fourth quarter was US$11,683, up 3%. The customer base is growing faster than what each customer pays. In April 2026 HubSpot moved its Customer Agent from US$1.00 per conversation to US$0.50 per resolved conversation, and its Prospecting Agent to US$1.00 per lead recommended for outreach. The meter was already there. What changed is what it counts, and the price per unit went down.

HubSpot's two numbers, side by side, make the ceiling question concrete. US$11,683 a year is about US$974 a month. At US$0.50 per resolved conversation, an agent reaches that figure at roughly 1,950 resolved conversations a month. Past that point, the agent bill is larger than what an average customer pays for the whole subscription. This is an average across all customers and says nothing about any one account. What it does show is how quickly a usage line can overtake a seat line if nobody has set a cap.

Budgeting for a subscription and budgeting for consumption are two different jobs

A subscription is a decision made once a year by one person. Consumption is a forecast, and a forecast needs an owner. A line that moves with usage needs a number next to it for the year, and someone willing to sign under that number. When a line item stops being a fixed annual figure and starts moving with usage, the approval needs that forecast before it needs anything else.

This is how a buyer can believe the product works and still not sign: the question has moved to who will put their name on a number that has no ceiling.

Change the budget line and the rhythm of review changes with it. A budget set in headcount and seats is settled when it is negotiated. A budget held against a forecast is checked against actual consumption as it comes in. A line that moves does not have to wait for renewal to be dealt with when it overshoots. For the seller, signing is the first gate, and the usage report that follows is the second.

A ceiling is what makes a usage line approvable

A consumption contract has a moment the sales cycle does not rehearse: the first bill, and whether it matches the business case. Higher, and the buyer has to go and explain it. Lower, and the likely reason is that nobody is using the tool.

The way through is to put a ceiling on the table before the buyer has to ask for one. The cap is not a concession, it is what makes the purchase approvable at all. HubSpot's move to a price per resolved conversation is the same problem seen from the seller's side: the meter now counts outcomes, and the buyer still has to say how many outcomes a year will hold.

What the mailbox rules cover, and what a customer logo proves

The story of AI flooding inboxes with outbound email is only half right. From 1 February 2024, Google required anyone sending more than 5,000 messages a day to personal Gmail accounts to authenticate with DMARC, keep spam rates below 0.30% and offer one click unsubscribe. Google's own guidance says the rules do not apply to mail sent to Google Workspace accounts, which is the kind of address B2B outbound goes to. The rules raised the cost of sending more to consumer inboxes, and left company domains outside their scope. An AI product whose value depends on volume has to be read against which inboxes it is sending to.

The clearest cautionary tale in this category is 11x. In March 2025 TechCrunch reported that ZoomInfo and Airtable were listed as customers on 11x's website without being customers; Airtable had run a very short trial. ZoomInfo, which sells AI sales tools of its own and whose lawyers had cited deceptive trade practices and trademark infringement in the same dispute, told TechCrunch: "During the pilot, 11x's product performed significantly worse than our SDR employees, and we did not move forward afterward."

For anyone reading a deal, that episode has a direct use. Every logo on a customer slide gets one question: paying customer, trial, or a conversation that happened once. The answer changes what the slide is worth, and founders who have nothing to hide answer it without being asked twice.

Sell the ceiling first

First, sell the ceiling before you sell the outcome. A buyer who cannot forecast the cost cannot get the approval, however good the outcome is. Bring the cap to the first meeting.

Second, find out which budget line the money comes from. A tool bought from a line that is fixed for the year and a tool bought from a line held against a forecast have different approval paths and different ceilings. The answer tells you who has to sign.

Third, treat adoption claims as claims. The only adoption figure in this piece backed by a named survey is that 1%. The 78%, the 4x and the 200% are Menlo's estimate and Clay's own numbers. The figures from ZoomInfo, HubSpot and Salesforce come from filings and earnings releases; the rest are the companies' own or a survey's estimate.

The category is real and someone will win it. The number that decides who wins is the one the buyer has to put in front of their CFO, with a ceiling on it.

The weakest number in this piece, named by us before someone else does

Read Menlo's text alone and the easiest conclusion is that sales has no line in the departmental budget, and an argument built on that gap falls over. The text skips sales in one list, but the chart puts sales at US$390 million, fifth of nine categories, about 5% of departmental AI, with 78% going to startups. The report names those startups as winning workflows Salesforce does not own. The author works at Salesforce, and a reader deserves to know that before weighing any of this.

Without that misreading, what still stands is narrower than the headline. Enterprises spent US$390 million on sales AI in the year Menlo measured, most of it with startups, and 1% of the B2B companies Bridge Group surveyed used an AI SDR. Bridge Group's respondents have a median revenue of US$47 million and are mostly North American software companies, so that 1% describes them, not large enterprises. What neither survey can show is why the sales line is small. The explanation offered here, that a usage-priced tool needs a ceiling before anyone signs, is an argument from how budgets get approved. Nothing in the two surveys proves it.

What to do this week, for nothing

No new tool, no pricing change. Three enterprise deals that stalled recently, and a sheet of paper.

For each one, write down three things. Which budget line the money would have come from. Who signs that line, by title, and by name if you can. And in which meeting you first gave a ceiling for a year, or whether you ever did. If two of the three deals have no name in the second column, the product is at most part of what stalled them.

Before the next meeting on the next deal, run the calculator above with that buyer's real volumes and take the number into the first meeting. It costs nothing and takes ten minutes.

Where EvoScale reads this

On a market-size page for sales AI, the part we can check is the one Menlo measured: what enterprises spent on it, by department, in a year. That number is US$390 million, and 78% of it already sits with startups. The page this piece asks for instead is the one a buyer has to write: a year's cost for the tool, with a ceiling, and the name of the person who signs it.

This lens has a blind spot. It rests on one survey and one vendor's pricing change, and it favours tools that need a signature from outside the sales team. Where the person who signs and the person who uses the tool are the same, the gate this piece describes does not exist, and a fund reading deals through this lens would pass on those tools for the wrong reason.
