Why Capable Teams Still Keep Cross-Border Hiring In House

The first cross-border hire usually gets budgeted like a vendor contract: sign an Employer of Record, payroll goes out, done. Is it that simple? Employing someone overseas means passing three tests: whether the local authority would call this person an employee, what the statutory cost is, and whether the worker's country can tax the company. An EOR takes over only the legal employer's share; the other two follow what the person actually does every day. Get the order wrong and the company usually finds out when the bill arrives.

The contract says contractor. Why is this person still an employee?

An experienced HR leader reads the risk list, understands every line, and still keeps the work in house. Why? Three reasons hold up, and none of them is ignorance of the rules. The volume is not there yet. Two people in one market cannot feed a vendor. In house looks free. Colleagues already on payroll absorb the cost, and not a dollar of it shows on the ledger. When something breaks, nobody inside can see it. Two quiet years look like proof. The day it flips is usually the day a second market opens, or something has to be corrected retroactively.

The correction turns on subordination. Most jurisdictions look at substance, and the question is who controls the work: who assigns it, who sets the hours, who carries the business risk. A contractor finds their own clients and carries their own profit and loss; someone who picks up tasks in your group chat every day is an employee, whatever the cover page says. A contractor agreement starts work with one signature and treats labour risk as zero, which is exactly why regulators test it first and a subordination test overturns it most often. Using it to save the cost of employment is a bet on how loose enforcement is in a country the company may never have visited.

Gross salary is only the starting price

On the cost layer, get two numbers straight before quoting a hiring cost to a founder. Under Article 14 of the Labor Pension Act, the employer contributes at least 6% of wages into the employee's pension account, on top of gross salary; under Article 27 of the National Health Insurance Act, the employer, as the insured unit, bears 60% of a salaried employee's premium. Both are floors set by statute, unrelated to what an EOR charges in fees; once the relationship is employment, they are paid under whichever structure the hire is made. Gross salary is the line the employee reads; add these two and you have the employer's real starting price.

Price the first overseas hire off gross salary and the budget is short by a statutory percentage the moment payroll runs. Reclassify the arrangement later from contractor to employee and it is short again: pension and insurance obligations reach back over the period the relationship existed, back payments plus interest, and the head of HR spends the next year explaining instead of hiring. Failing to enrol someone is fined on top: four times the employer's share of labour insurance premiums, ten times for employment insurance, both counted from the day of hire. The tab starts running on day one; the day it is caught is just when it gets settled.

How does one remote employee become a branch office with no sign on the door?

Tax law calls this permanent establishment. Under Article 5 of the OECD Model Tax Convention, a person habitually concluding contracts on the company's behalf can create one, and in the tax office's eyes the company has opened a branch there, just without a sign on the door. An EOR only puts a local legal employer between the company and the tax authority; what the person does every day does not change. Set the three structures side by side: a local entity takes months, the company carries every obligation itself, and there is no misclassification question; an EOR is live in days to weeks, takes on labour compliance as the legal employer, and lowers the permanent establishment exposure, though the work itself still decides.

Change the country, and the whole labour-law encyclopaedia starts over

Japan is the clearest case: company registration, social insurance filings and payroll each sit with a different profession, and the social insurance filings are reserved work for a licensed consultant. So what happens to a Taiwanese company landing there? It ends up hiring several of them at once, and the question stops being which vendor and becomes how many, and who holds the thread between them. It is a relay team: every runner is fast, and the baton is always dropped at the handover. All of this has to be settled before the first payslip exists, and the list is rewritten every time the country changes.

Everyone talks about recruiting when they talk about going overseas. But past recruiting, it is the employment itself where the devil is in the detail.

The real cost of a cross-border hire is decided by where the responsibility lands

Hiring across borders is worth doing, and it is an adventure full of opportunity, as long as the order is right the first time: ask whether the local authority would call this person an employee, then work out the statutory floor, then ask the tax question, and run payroll after all three. The price of that order is a slightly later first start date. The bill for getting it wrong does not arrive on the day of the mistake; it arrives on the day someone finally reads the contract closely, usually much later, and much more expensive. Take your own onboarding flow and check it against this once.
