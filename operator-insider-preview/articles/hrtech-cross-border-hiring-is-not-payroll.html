Why Capable Teams Still Keep Cross-Border Hiring In House

Operator Insider · HRTech · 6 min read · September 2026
Operator Insider · HRTech

Deputy General Manager, BIPO Taiwan

LinkedIn profile

A startup's first cross-border hire usually gets budgeted like a vendor contract: pick an Employer of Record, sign, and the payroll problem is considered solved. What has to be handled is three separate exposures: whether the employment relationship is compliant, whether the arrangement creates a tax obligation in the worker's country, and whether a local labor authority would call this person an employee. Get the order wrong and the company usually finds out when the bill arrives.

What changes the answer is not a new rule

The decision usually flips for one of two reasons. The first is that the second market arrives: one country in house is a task, three countries is a function nobody was hired to run. The second is that something has to be corrected retroactively. A misclassification does not cost what it would have cost to do it properly. It costs the back payments, plus the interest, plus the year in which the head of HR is explaining rather than hiring.

The test that ignores what the contract is titled

A competent HR leader reads the list of risks, understands every item on it, and still decides to keep the work in house. Why? Three reasons hold up, and none of them is ignorance of the rules. The volume is not there yet. Two people in one market does not justify a vendor. Control feels cheaper than it is. The cost is absorbed by people already on payroll, so it looks free. The failure mode is invisible from the inside. Nothing goes wrong for two years, and two years of nothing feels like proof.

Most jurisdictions run some version of the same substance-over-form test, and the underlying question is always who controls the work. Using a contractor structure to avoid the cost of employment is a bet on enforcement risk in a country the company may not have visited.

Each multiple has its own base, stated in the statute; they are not multiples of salary. Where one act breaches several duties, Article 24 of the Administrative Penalty Act applies the highest single fine rather than adding them up.

The cost floor that shows up after the pilot, not during it

Two numbers are worth knowing before quoting a hiring cost to a founder. Under Article 14 of the Labor Pension Act, an employer must contribute at least 6% of an employee's monthly wage into that employee's individual pension account, on top of gross salary. Under Article 27 of the National Health Insurance Act, the employer, as the insured unit, bears 60% of a salaried employee's premium. Both are a floor set by statute, unrelated to whatever an EOR charges, and they apply whichever structure the hire is made through.

Price the first hire off the gross salary line and the budget is short by a statutory percentage the moment payroll runs, and short again if the arrangement is later reclassified from contractor to employee, because the pension and insurance obligations apply retroactively to the period the relationship existed.

One remote employee can create a taxable presence abroad

The third layer is tax, and international tax law calls it permanent establishment. Under Article 5 of the OECD Model Tax Convention, a person habitually concluding contracts on the company's behalf can create an agency permanent establishment. What an EOR does is put a local legal employer between the company and the tax authority; it does not change what the worker does day to day, and permanent establishment turns on function and authority.

The misclassification row works the same way. Where the company is directing daily tasks and setting hours while calling the worker a contractor on paper, an EOR contract does not fix the relationship; it just changes who is holding it.

Change country and it is not the rate that changes

Cross a border and it is the table itself that changes. Japan is the clearest case: company registration, social insurance filings and payroll each sit with a different kind of professional there, and filing the statutory social insurance paperwork is reserved work for a licensed consultant. A Taiwanese company ends up contracting several of them, and the question stops being which vendor to use and becomes how many, and who holds the thread between them. All of it is settled before the first payslip exists.

Everyone talks about recruiting when they talk about going overseas. But past recruiting, it is the employment itself where the devil is in the detail.

Sabrina Chiu, LinkedIn, April 2023
An employer of record lets a company test a market without standing up an entity first. That is a good reason to use one. It is not a reason to skip the question of who handles the local filings, because a platform that only puts your hire on its own books leaves you looking for a second vendor to do everything else.

The weakest number in this piece, named by us before someone else does

The 14.87 percent carries more of this article than any other figure, and it has two problems. The first is that it is low. National Health Insurance is not in it, and on the published 2026 table the employer's share at an insured salary of NT$45,800 is NT$2,216, about 4.84 points; add the wage arrears fund under the Labor Standards Act and the real figure is close to a fifth. So 14.87 per cent is a floor, not the bill.

The second problem is bigger: the denominator. All four figures describe someone insured in Taiwan, while this piece is about hiring across a border; hiring a local person in another country swaps in that country's table. What to take away is a habit: there is a statutory load of roughly a fifth on top of gross pay, and it gets worked out again in every country.

What to do this week, for nothing

First, look up the industry classification your company filed for its employees and read it against the 2026 occupational injury rate table. The full spread across the 55 classes reaches eight times, but the neighbouring classes a software company could land in sit about 0.03 points apart, so the check is whether the box is right. That box was filled in once by whoever did the paperwork, and almost nobody goes back to it.

Second, take one contractor or consultant agreement that is running right now and put the three subordination questions to it: who decides the hours and the place of work, whether the pay is tied to a deliverable or to time, and who supplies the equipment and carries the expenses. The Ministry of Labor's employment-relationship checklist runs to 25 items, and it states that the assessment is made on the whole picture, with no threshold of so many answers. Work through the three questions, then take the checklist across the whole agreement. After that the title on the front page is not the point.

Where EvoScale reads this

Operator-led investing reads a hiring plan by its order of operations. When legal classification and statutory cost are checked only after a hire is already live, it is usually because someone has asked why the payroll run does not match the offer letter. The labor law question and the tax question belong ahead of the payroll question, and the price of that order is a later first start date.

For a HRTech company selling into this problem, the product worth underwriting is not the payroll dashboard. The defensible layer is the sequencing logic itself: which jurisdiction's subordination test applies, which permanent establishment trigger the role could hit, and how the statutory floor changes the moment a headcount plan crosses a border. That is hard to fake and hard to copy quickly.

None of this argues against hiring across borders; it argues for pricing the decision correctly the first time. The invoice for getting it wrong rarely comes due on the day of the mistake. It comes due on the day someone finally reads the contract closely, usually much later, and much more expensive.
