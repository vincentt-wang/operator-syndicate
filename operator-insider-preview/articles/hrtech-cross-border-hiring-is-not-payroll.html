Cross-border hiring: why capable teams still do it themselves

A first cross-border hire usually gets budgeted like any vendor contract: find an Employer of Record, get payroll out, done. Is it really that simple? Hiring overseas has to pass three tests: whether the local authority would call this person an employee, what the statutory cost is, and whether their country can tax the company. An EOR takes over only the named-employer layer. The other two follow what this person actually does every day.

The contract says contractor. Why is this person still an employee?

An experienced HR leader reads the risk list and still keeps the work in house. Three reasons hold up, and none of them is ignorance of the rules.

Future cost
With one or two people in a market, a monthly EOR fee is genuinely good value. As the team grows the headcount doubles and so does the fee line. There is no economy of scale in it.
In house looks free
The cost is absorbed by colleagues already on payroll, so none of it shows up on the ledger.
Risk you cannot see
Two quiet years look like proof that it worked. The day it gets reopened is usually the day a second market launches, or an inherited problem meets an audit.

Cross-border contracting and employer-of-record services are common business models worldwide, but neither is a universal compliance cure. Most jurisdictions look at how the work is actually performed: who controls it, who assigns it, who sets the hours, who carries the business risk. If the person is an independent consultant running their own profit and loss and delivering against outcomes, contracting is entirely lawful. If they pick up work in the company group chat every day under day-to-day direction, they are an employee, whatever the cover page says. Using a contractor label to save on employment cost puts the company's compliance at risk in a country it may never have visited.

Base salary is only the starting price

On the cost layer, get two numbers straight before taking a figure to the founder. Taking a local Taiwanese hire as the example, Article 14 of the Labor Pension Act requires the employer to contribute at least 6% of wages into a pension account on top of salary, with labour and health insurance on top of that. Hire staff based overseas and the statutory employer load runs somewhere between 15 and 30 per cent depending on the country. These are statutory floors, unrelated to what an EOR charges in fees. Once the relationship counts as employment, no structure avoids them. Base salary is the line the employee reads; add the employer's share and you have the real starting price.

Price that first overseas hire off base salary alone, engage them as a cross-border contractor, and a later reclassification to employment does real damage: pension and insurance obligations reach back across the entire period the relationship existed, with back payments and interest, and the head of HR spends the next year explaining it upwards. Failure to enrol carries its own penalty; in Taiwan it is four times the employer's share of labour insurance premiums and ten times for employment insurance, all counted from the start date. The tab has been running since day one. The day it is caught is simply when it gets settled.

How does one remote employee become a branch office with no sign on the door?

Tax law calls this permanent establishment. Under Article 5 of the OECD Model Tax Convention, someone who habitually negotiates or concludes binding contracts on the company's behalf can create one, and in the local tax office's eyes the company has opened a branch there without putting a sign on the door. Some companies assume an EOR removes the tax exposure, but an EOR only places a local legal employer between the company and the tax authority; what that person does every day does not change. If they are closing deals for headquarters every week, the tax office will still come looking.

Change the country, and the whole labour-law encyclopaedia starts over

Japan is the clearest case. Company registration, social insurance filings and payroll each sit with a different licensed profession: the judicial scrivener, the labour and social security attorney, and the tax accountant. Social insurance filings are the exclusive work of the second of those, and an ordinary HR firm cannot file them on your behalf. So what happens when a Taiwanese company lands there? It ends up onboarding several vendors at once, and the question moves quickly from which one to how many, and who holds the line between them. It is a relay team: every runner is quick, and the baton is always dropped at the handover. All of it has to be settled before the first payslip exists, and the list is rewritten every time the country changes.

Everyone talks about recruiting when they talk about going overseas. But past recruiting, it is the employment itself where the devil is in the detail.

Compliance and payroll run in parallel, not in a queue

Hiring across borders is something any company expanding overseas has to do, and it is an adventure, as long as the commercial push and the compliance structure are costed at the same time. Speed is what wins people and markets, so the compliance work runs alongside onboarding and payroll rather than queueing in front of it. Getting the order wrong is not something you feel at the time; the bill arrives on the day something breaks, and it is usually a much larger one. Four things start at once:

  1. ClassificationSettle whether the local authority treats this role as employment or contracting, while you are still discussing salary
  2. The floorPut the local statutory load and any EOR fee into the same headcount budget
  3. Taxing rightsDraw the limits of what this person may sign, so you do not create a permanent establishment or a joint-employer problem by accident
  4. StructurePick the structure that fits, contracting, EOR or an entity, and only then run onboarding and payroll
