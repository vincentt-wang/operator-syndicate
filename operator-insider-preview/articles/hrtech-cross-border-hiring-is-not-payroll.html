Why Capable Teams Still Keep Cross-Border Hiring In House

Operator Insider · HRTech · 10 min read · September 2026
Operator Insider · HRTech

Deputy General Manager, BIPO Taiwan

LinkedIn profile

A startup's first cross-border hire usually gets budgeted like a vendor contract: pick an Employer of Record, sign, and the payroll problem is considered solved. What the EOR stands between the company and is three separate exposures, whether the work sits inside a compliant employment relationship, whether the arrangement creates a tax obligation in the worker's country, and whether a labor authority would call this person an employee no matter what the paperwork says. Get the order wrong and the company finds out after the bill for it arrives, not before.

What changes the answer is not a new rule

The decision usually flips for one of two reasons, and neither is a regulatory change. The first is that the second market arrives. One country in house is a task; three countries in house is a function nobody was hired to run. The second is that something is corrected retroactively. A misclassification does not cost what it would have cost to do it properly. It costs the back payments, plus the interest, plus the year in which the head of HR is explaining rather than hiring.

Which is why the list below is worth reading even if you have already decided to keep it in house. It is not an argument for outsourcing. It is the set of things that determine what your own decision will cost you if it turns out to be wrong.

The test that ignores what the contract is titled

Every guide to cross-border hiring is a list of risks. The lists are accurate and they are also easy to write, which is why there are so many of them. The question they never answer is the one that decides the purchase: a competent HR leader reads that same list, understands every item on it, and still decides to keep the work in house. Why?

Three reasons hold up, and none of them is ignorance of the rules. The volume is not there yet. Two people in one market does not justify a vendor relationship, and the person deciding knows it. Control feels cheaper than it is. Keeping it in house looks free because the cost is absorbed by people already on payroll. The failure mode is invisible from the inside. Nothing goes wrong for two years, and two years of nothing feels like proof.

This is not a uniquely Taiwanese habit. Most jurisdictions that regulate employment run some version of the same substance-over-form test, they differ in name and in how aggressively regulators enforce it, but the underlying question, who controls the work, is close to universal. A company using a contractor structure to avoid the cost of employment is making a bet on enforcement risk in a country it may not have visited.

The cost floor that shows up after the pilot, not during it

Two numbers in Taiwan law are worth knowing before quoting a hiring cost to a founder. Under Article 14 of the Labor Pension Act, an employer must contribute at least 6% of an employee's monthly wage into that employee's individual pension account, on top of gross salary. Under Article 27 of the National Health Insurance Act, for an ordinary salaried employee the employer, as the insured unit, bears 60% of the National Health Insurance premium, the employee pays 30 percent and central government covers the remaining 10 percent. Neither number is a service fee an EOR charges. Both are a floor set by statute, and they apply whether the hire is made through a local entity, an EOR, or gets misclassified as a contractor and later reclassified.

The reason this matters for sequencing is that a startup pricing its first hire off the gross salary line will be short by a predictable, statutory percentage the moment payroll runs, and short again if the arrangement is later reclassified from contractor to employee, because the pension and insurance obligations apply retroactively to the period the relationship existed, not from the date it was corrected.

One remote employee can create a taxable presence abroad

The third layer has a formal name in international tax law: permanent establishment. Article 5 of the OECD Model Tax Convention on Income and on Capital sets out when a foreign company's presence in a country becomes a taxable one, and one of the routes it lists, the agency permanent establishment under Article 5, paragraphs 5 and 6, is triggered when a person habitually concludes contracts on the company's behalf, regardless of whether that company ever rented an office. A remote employee closing deals, signing local agreements, or acting with authority on the company's behalf can create exactly this kind of taxable presence in the country where they sit.

An EOR is often marketed as removing this risk entirely. What it does is put a local legal employer between the company and the tax authority, it does not change what the worker does day to day, and agency permanent establishment turns on function and authority, not on who signs the paycheck. A company whose remote hire is closing contracts, not just doing back-office work, should treat the tax question and the labor law question as two separate diligence items, because a structure that solves one does not automatically solve the other.

The three common hiring structures each put the risk somewhere different. A local entity puts every layer of risk on the company but also gives it full control. An EOR is supposed to absorb the labor law and payroll layer by standing in as the legal employer, but it does not erase a badly structured relationship, it only relabels who is holding it. An independent contractor arrangement looks like it avoids all three risks, that is exactly why founders reach for it first, and it is also the structure regulators are trained to test first.

In the misclassification row, the answer depends on what the work looks like day to day, whichever vendor the company picks. An EOR contract does not fix a relationship where the company is directing daily tasks, setting hours, and integrating the worker into internal systems while calling them a contractor on paper. It just moves that same fact pattern onto a different legal employer.

Change country and it is not the rate that changes

At least the Taiwanese table is public: the insured-salary brackets move with the minimum wage each year, while the rates themselves are reviewed every two years for labour insurance and every three for injury insurance. What makes this hard is that crossing a border does not change the numbers on that table. It changes which table exists.

Japan is the clearest case. Company registration, social insurance filings and payroll each sit with a different kind of professional there. Filing the statutory social insurance paperwork is reserved work for a licensed consultant; running the payroll calculation is not. In practice a Taiwanese company ends up contracting several of them, and the question stops being which vendor to use and becomes how many, and who holds the thread between them.

Recruiting there runs on a different rail as well. Intermediaries carry far more of the market than they do here, and a role posted directly on a platform brings back very little. Budget for roughly double what the same seniority costs in Taiwan. And because long tenure is still the norm, anything that reads to a candidate as a non-permanent arrangement is a harder sell there than here, which is worth knowing before you plan how to present the offer.

Thailand runs on its own rail. Pay cycles come up in the first conversation rather than the last, and more than one market in the region runs on something other than a single monthly run. Several markets in the region still require paper filings in person. Not every country has online enrolment.

None of these is a payroll question. Every one of them is settled before the first payslip exists.

Everyone talks about recruiting when they talk about going overseas. But past recruiting, it is the employment itself where the devil is in the detail.

Sabrina Chiu, LinkedIn, April 2023
The point of an employer-of-record arrangement is to let a company test a market without standing up an entity first. That is a good reason to use one. It is not a reason to skip the question of who handles the local filings, because a platform that only puts your hire on its own books leaves you looking for a second vendor to do everything else.

So the first question is whether this is a trial or a plan. Which country comes after that. A trial genuinely suits an employer of record. A plan does not, and the two get confused because the paperwork looks the same in month one.

The weakest number in this piece, named by us before someone else does

The 14.87 percent carries more of this article than any other figure, and it has two problems.

The first problem is that it is low. National Health Insurance is not in it, and the published 2026 table does exist: at an insured salary of NT$45,800 the employer's share is NT$2,216, about 4.84 points. Add the wage arrears fund under the Labor Standards Act and the real figure is close to a fifth. So 14.87 per cent is a floor, not the bill.

The second problem is bigger: the denominator. All four figures describe someone insured in Taiwan, while this piece is about hiring across a border. A company hiring in Taiwan uses the table directly. Staff posted abroad can stay on labour insurance under Article 9 of the Labor Insurance Act. Hiring a local person in another country swaps in that country’s table, and none of the Taiwanese brackets carries over. What to take away is a habit: there is a statutory load of roughly a fifth on top of gross pay, and it gets worked out again in every country.

What to do this week, for nothing

No lawyer, no system change, no vendor call. Two documents you already have and one public table.

First, look up the industry classification your company filed for its own employees and read it against the 115 occupational injury rate table. The full spread across the 55 classes reaches eight times, but the neighbouring classes a software company could land in sit about 0.03 points apart, so the check is whether the box is right. That box was filled in once by whoever did the paperwork, and almost nobody goes back to it. If it is wrong, ask the Bureau of Labor Insurance how to correct it.

Second, take one contractor or consultant agreement that is running right now and put the three subordination questions to it. Who decides the hours and the place of work. Is the pay tied to a deliverable or to time. Who supplies the equipment and carries the expenses. The Ministry of Labor's employment-relationship checklist runs to 25 items in four groups covering personal, economic and organisational subordination plus further indicators, and it states that the assessment is made on the whole picture, with no threshold of so many answers. Work through the three questions, then take the checklist across the whole agreement. After that the title on the front page is not the point.

Both take one afternoon, and both sit on the Taiwanese side. In another country the same two questions have to be asked again against that country’s table, and that is where the spending starts.

Where EvoScale reads this

Operator-led investing reads a hiring plan by its order of operations. When legal classification and statutory cost are checked only after a hire is already live, it is usually because someone has asked why the payroll run does not match the offer letter. The labor law question and the tax question belong ahead of the payroll question, before the first cross-border offer letter goes out. That order has a price of its own: asking those questions first pushes back the first start date.

For a HRTech company selling into this problem, the product worth underwriting is not the payroll dashboard, dashboards are commoditized and every competitor has one. The defensible layer is the sequencing logic itself: knowing which jurisdiction's subordination test applies, which permanent establishment trigger the role in question could hit, and which statutory floor changes the moment a headcount plan crosses a border. That knowledge does not show up on a feature comparison chart, and it is exactly the kind of operating detail that is hard to fake and hard to copy quickly.

None of this argues against hiring across borders, it argues for pricing the whole decision correctly the first time. A founder who treats an EOR as a payroll vendor will still get a functioning payroll run. What they will not get, unless they ask for it specifically, is a clear answer on which labor law applies, whether the role creates a taxable presence somewhere else, and whether the underlying relationship would survive a subordination test if anyone ever looked closely. Those three questions cost nothing to ask before the offer letter goes out.

The category is not glamorous, and the invoice for getting it wrong rarely comes due on the day of the mistake. It comes due on the day someone finally reads the contract closely, and that is usually much later, and much more expensive, than the day it was signed.
