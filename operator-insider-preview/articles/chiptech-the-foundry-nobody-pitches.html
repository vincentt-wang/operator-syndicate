Selling to a Customer Who Has Already Decided

A hardware team can spend two years building a chip that works and still fail, because no fab will put it into production on a timeline the company can survive. Taiwan's funding conversation rarely gets that far. It stops at whether TSMC will take the call.

Advanced nodes are 77% of TSMC's wafer revenue

TSMC's Q4 2025 earnings call put a number on where the industry's attention sits. Nodes at 7 nanometers and below accounted for 77% of that quarter's wafer revenue: 28% from 3nm, 35% from 5nm, 14% from 7nm. That is the business TSMC is built to run, and it is the business every Taiwanese pitch deck assumes when it says we are talking to TSMC.

Inside that 77%, 5nm still out-earned 3nm, 35% against 28%, and 2nm had only just entered volume production in the fourth quarter. 3nm went from 24% of wafer revenue for full-year 2025 to 28% in the fourth quarter and 30% in the second quarter of 2026; 5nm eased from 36% for the year to 33%; 2nm was 3% by the second quarter. A new node takes several quarters after volume production before it overtakes the one before it, and until then the previous generation is still the one earning the most. The remaining 23% is every node more mature than 7nm added together, at the largest foundry in the world. Twelve of those points are 16nm to 28nm, mostly general-purpose logic and not specialty at all; 40/45nm is 3%, 65nm is 4%, and 90nm and older add up to 4%. Together they are the part a Taiwanese pitch almost never mentions.

On the same call, CFO Wendell Huang laid out where TSMC's 2026 capital budget goes. The January guidance was US$52 to 56 billion: about 70% to 80% into advanced process technologies, 10% to 20% into advanced packaging, testing and mask making, and about 10% into specialty technologies. On July 16, according to media reports, TSMC raised the full-year budget to US$60 to 64 billion and left those proportions unchanged. The top of the packaging, testing and mask range is twice the specialty share, and specialty is the only one of the three given as a single figure with no range. At the world's largest foundry, the specialty processes a hardware startup runs into, RF, power and sensors, share one tenth of the plan.

On the January guidance that tenth is US$5.2 to 5.6 billion; after the July revision, US$6.0 to 6.4 billion. GlobalFoundries, where I work, spent US$722 million on capital expenditure in 2025, the gross figure in the annual report; the net figure reported on the earnings call was US$574 million. That was also the low point of the last three years, against US$1.804 billion in 2023, and the 2026 guidance is net capex of 15% to 20% of revenue. Measured against the 2025 gross figure, TSMC's tenth is seven to nine times GlobalFoundries' entire capital spend; measured against 2023, it is about three times. What the tenth measures is priority. It tells you where specialty sits on the leader's list, and for a startup trying to get capacity, the ranking is what matters.

A specialist's whole year is under a quarter of the leader's average quarter

TSMC's 2025 revenue was about US$122 billion, so an average quarter is about US$30.5 billion. GlobalFoundries' full year was US$6.79 billion, 22% of that quarter. Specialty is a separate business, and its size is measured with a different ruler. A specialty foundry matters to a hardware startup if it has the right process family, the capacity to run it, and a commercial team that will take the call. Those three things are not correlated with size.

GlobalFoundries in 2025: mobile shrank, automotive and datacenter grew

The specialty side is usually described as automotive and IoT. GlobalFoundries' 2025 annual report breaks that description into parts. Revenue for the year was US$6.79 billion, up 0.6%, which the press release rounds to 1%. Automotive was US$1.41 billion, 21% of total revenue, up 16.9%, which the report attributes to content expansion, diversification and share gains. Communications infrastructure and datacenter was US$745 million, up 29.1% for the year and 32% in the fourth quarter alone, driven by silicon photonics and satellite communications. The AI build-out buys optical interconnect and communications silicon as well as leading-edge logic, and none of that is made on the nodes the funding conversation is about.

The company grew 0.6% because two segments shrank: smart mobile devices, US$2.68 billion, down 12.1%, and home and industrial IoT, US$1.19 billion, down 6.2%. The US$370 million mobile lost is almost exactly the US$372 million automotive and datacenter gained together. A startup pitching a phone chip to a specialty foundry walks into the part that is shrinking; one pitching automotive walks into the part that grew the most. The door is open in both cases, and what the foundry hopes to hear is different in each.

One more line is easy to miss: non-wafer revenue, US$769 million, up 17.9%, about the same growth rate as automotive. Strip it out and GlobalFoundries' 2025 wafer revenue is US$6.02 billion, which puts automotive at 23.4% of wafer revenue against 21% of total revenue. The same segment moves by more than two points depending on the denominator, so when you read any foundry's end-market split, ask first whether the base is total revenue or wafer revenue.

This matters for anyone underwriting a hardware deal because it changes which question is diagnostic. Asking a team whether they are on an advanced node tells you very little. Asking which family their physics requires, and whether anyone at that fab has returned their call, tells you whether the chip ships.

Mature-node capacity stopped being slack in 2026

In October 2024, TrendForce forecast that average utilization at 28nm and above would stay below 80%, recovering only slightly to above 75% in 2025, while 5/4nm and 3nm ran full through the end of 2024. The idea that the other side of the line has room is a 2024 forecast, and it should be read as one.

By 2026 that premise no longer holds as stated. TrendForce's May 7, 2026 release projects average 8-inch utilization across the top ten foundries approaching 90% this year, up from about 80% in 2025, while 12-inch mature nodes are not yet in severe shortage. Its June 30 release reports foundry prices up across the board between the first and second quarters of 2026, by 5% to 15% on average, with a third round of increases being prepared and 12-inch mature nodes gradually recovering. The same release points to production cuts at TSMC and Samsung, and expects TSMC's cuts to move orders to other foundries over the medium to long term.

For a small customer the two states of the market pull in opposite directions. When lines are slack you can get a meeting and a discount. When they tighten, the discount goes first and the slot gets harder to hold. The 5% to 15% TrendForce reports is a price move; whether the schedule still has your name on it is something no press release will report. What survives both states is an engineering relationship: a named application engineer who already knows your design does not disappear when the discount does. That is the thing to lock in, whichever side of the cycle you are pitching into.

The physics picks the node before the term sheet does

For a hardware startup, which side of that line its chip sits on is decided early, usually before the term sheet, by the physics of what the product does. RF front ends run on RF-SOI or SiGe, power management ICs on BCD, low-power edge silicon on FD-SOI, because those processes are the right physical fit, and a cheaper version of an advanced node would not do the same job. A team that has already scoped which process family its die needs, and lined up a fab relationship for it, has done a piece of diligence that a slide about a roadmap to TSMC does not replace.

Ask about the node before the market size

First, the fab question belongs in the first diligence call, not the technical annex. Ask which process node the die needs and why, before asking about market size. Second, do not read a mention of TSMC as a maturity signal on its own. For a large share of hardware categories, the correct answer to the process question is a foundry that does not appear on the deck. Third, treat specialty foundry relationships the way you already treat channel partnerships: a form of go-to-market risk that sits outside the product roadmap slide, and one worth asking about directly.

There is a version of this problem that has nothing to do with semiconductors. If you sell anything into a market where the customer already has a default answer, the first meeting is not about your specification. It is about working out whether they want a second source, or a lever to move the first one. Those two conversations look identical for about twenty minutes, and then they stop looking identical at all.

A customer looking for a second source asks about qualification data, reliability and how long it takes to get you onto the approved list. A customer looking for leverage asks for your price before the technical questions are finished, because the price is what they intend to carry back to the incumbent. If the price question arrives before the data question, are you the lever? Being the lever has a value of its own, but it is a different deal, and it should be priced and resourced as one. GlobalFoundries' own 2025 annual report shows what that deal looks like from the supplier's side: smart mobile revenue fell 12.1%, and one of the two reasons the report gives is, in its own words, pricing adjustments where customers are dual sourced. Average selling prices across the company fell 10.4%.

The weakest number in this piece, named by us before someone else does

GlobalFoundries' totals invite a wrong conclusion: the company grew about 1%, one segment grew 32%, so everything else shrank, and therefore a startup pitching an automotive chip to a specialty foundry is walking into the part of the business that is not expanding. The arithmetic is right and the conclusion is wrong. In the 2025 annual report the segments that shrank are smart mobile, down 12.1%, and IoT, down 6.2%. Automotive grew 16.9%, and its US$204 million gain is larger than datacenter's US$168 million. A total only tells you about the total, which is why this piece uses the segment figures from the report and labels the 32% as a single quarter.

The second weak spot is the tenth. A tenth of TSMC's capital budget sounds small. In dollars it is US$5.2 to 5.6 billion on the January guidance and US$6.0 to 6.4 billion after July, seven to nine times GlobalFoundries' gross 2025 capital expenditure of US$722 million. Change the denominator and the multiple moves from three to eleven: on the US$574 million net figure from the earnings call it is nine to eleven times, and on the 2023 figure of US$1.804 billion it is about three. What the tenth proves is where specialty sits on the leader's list. It proves nothing about whether specialty is neglected.

What still stands once that inference is gone: the fab question belongs in the first diligence call, and mature-node capacity tightened in 2026, so an engineering relationship is worth more than a discount. Neither depends on the claim that most hardware startups need specialty processes, a claim this piece does not make and has no demand-side data for.

What to do this week, for nothing

If you are building the chip, ask your target customer when their next system design freezes. Asking costs nothing. Then find a team that has already taped out on the same process and ask how long product qualification took them; the multi-project wafer (MPW) shuttle schedules that foundries publish tell you when the next run is. Put both numbers into the calculator above. If qualification is longer than the time to freeze, stop pitching this design cycle and start pitching the next one.

If you are reading the deal, ask in the first meeting which foundry has returned the team's call, and who at that foundry it was. The answer takes ten seconds and tells you which side of the line this company is really on.

Where EvoScale reads this

Operator-led investing means the earliest useful question is often the most boring one. For hardware and deep-tech deals, that question is which process family the die needs and whether the founding team has already had that conversation with a fab. Whether the deck can name TSMC does not answer it. A team that has mapped its production path onto a node it can tape out on has already removed the part of the risk a spreadsheet cannot see.

The category is not glamorous and it will not trend on a fundraising deck. It is the line that decides which chip reaches volume and which one stays a very expensive prototype.

This lens has a fair objection. Someone on the chip design side could say it is too early to talk foundries in a first meeting, because capacity is a waste of time before the product has found its market. For a product whose value is defined in software, that objection holds. Once the physics has already chosen the process, it holds less, and how early that happens is specific to the chip in question.
