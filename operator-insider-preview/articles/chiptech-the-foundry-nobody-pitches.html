Selling to a Customer Who Has Already Decided

A hardware team can spend two years getting silicon back that works and still fail, because no foundry will put it into volume inside the time the company has.

Advanced nodes are 77% of TSMC's wafer revenue

TSMC's Q4 2025 call shows where the attention sits. Nodes at 7nm and below produced 77 per cent of wafer revenue that quarter: 5nm 35 per cent, 3nm 28 per cent, 7nm 14 per cent. This is the business every deck assumes when it says it is talking to TSMC.

The other 23 per cent is every process older than 7nm at the largest foundry in the world, added together. Twelve points are 16 to 28nm, mostly general logic rather than specialty; 40/45nm is 3 per cent, 65nm 4 per cent, everything at 90nm and above another 4 per cent. Together they are the part Taiwanese decks almost never mention.

The same call broke down where 2026 capital spending goes: roughly 70 to 80 per cent into advanced nodes, 10 to 20 per cent into advanced packaging, testing and masks, and about 10 per cent into specialty. July's revision to US$60bn to US$64bn left the split unchanged, on a secondary source. That tenth is about US$6bn; GlobalFoundries spent US$722m gross across the whole company in 2025.

That tenth measures priority: it tells you where specialty sits on the leader's list. For a startup trying to secure capacity, the position on that list is the point, and whether a specialty foundry is useful comes down to the right process family, available capacity, and a commercial team that answers. None of the three tracks size.

GlobalFoundries in 2025: mobile shrank, automotive and datacenter grew

Specialty usually gets described as automotive and IoT; the 2025 annual report splits it finer. Revenue was US$6.79bn, up 0.6 per cent. Automotive was US$1.41 billion, up 16.9 per cent. Communications infrastructure and datacentre was US$745m, up 29.1 per cent, mostly silicon photonics and satellite. This AI build-out buys optical interconnect and communications silicon as well as advanced logic, and none of it is made on the nodes the fundraising conversation talks about.

The company grew 0.6 per cent because two lines shrank: smartphones down 12.1 per cent, home and industrial IoT down 6.2 per cent. The US$370m lost in handsets is almost exactly the US$372m added by automotive and datacentre. Take a handset chip to a specialty foundry and you walk into the part that is shrinking; take automotive and you walk into the part growing fastest.

This swaps in the question that discriminates. Asking which process family the physics requires, and whether anyone at that foundry returned the call, tells you whether the chip reaches volume.

Mature-node capacity stopped being slack in 2026

In October 2024 TrendForce forecast average utilisation at 28nm and above staying below 80 per cent. The sense that there is room on the other side of the line comes from a 2024 forecast.

By 2026 that no longer holds. TrendForce's May 2026 estimate puts average 8-inch utilisation at the top ten approaching 90 per cent, up clearly from about 80 per cent in 2025, and its June release reports foundry quotes up 5 to 15 per cent with a third round being prepared.

The two conditions pull opposite ways for a small customer: loose lines get you the meeting and the discount, tight lines take the discount first and the schedule stops being safe. What survives both is one application engineer who knows your design and whose name you know.

The physics picks the node before the term sheet does

Which side a die lands on is usually settled by physics before any term sheet. RF front ends go on RF-SOI or SiGe, power management on BCD, low-power edge parts on FD-SOI, because those are the physically correct choices and a cheaper advanced node cannot do the same job. A team that knows its process family and opened the foundry conversation early has done diligence no deck replaces.

Whether a chip reaches volume is decided by its production path, not by its spec sheet

For hardware and deep tech the most useful question is often the least interesting one: which process family does this die need, and has the team actually talked to a foundry. It will never appear on a trend slide, but it is the line that decides which chip reaches volume and which one stops at an expensive prototype. On the next hardware deal, put process and qualification months into the first conversation instead of the technical annex.
